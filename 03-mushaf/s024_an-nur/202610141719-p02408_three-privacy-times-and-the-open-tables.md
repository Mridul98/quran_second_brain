# Three privacy times and the open tables (24:58–61)
**UID:** 202610141719
**Type:** Passage / Ruku Note
**Passage:** 24:58–61 (Ruku 8 of 9 in an-Nur)
**Last Studied:** 2026-10-14

## 1. Unit & Thesis
* **Range:** `[24:58]` – `[24:61]` (4 ayahs)
* **Thesis:** The permission law matures into domestic scheduling: dependents and pre-pubescent children seek entry at three vulnerable hours — before dawn, noon-change, after night prayer — because privacy is time-bound; at puberty the children join the grown-ups' rule (58–59); elderly women take the leniency of the outer garment while modesty stays "better" (60); and the exempted (blind, lame, sick) plus the extended-family and friend tables open completely — "eating together or separately" with God-greeting at every door (61).
* **Placement:** Ruku 8; the applied re-run of ruku 4's threshold-law (27–29) now for the whole household calendar, closing before the assembly-permission of ruku 9.

## 2. Verse Arc (corpus-anchored)
* **`[24:58]`** "O you who believe! Permission must be requested by your servants and those of you who have not reached puberty. On three occasions: before the Dawn Prayer, and at noon when you change your clothes, and after the Evening Prayer. These are three occasions of privacy for you. At other times, it is not wrong for you or them to intermingle with one another. Allah thus clarifies the revelations for you. Allah is Knowledgeable and Wise."
  * **Gloss:** The three ʿawra-times: sleep, undress, and rest-hours define when dependents and minors must knock; outside them, free intermixture — privacy as a clock, not a wall; the kalam-clause "Allah thus clarifies" bookends the verse (and again at 59).
* **`[24:59]`** "When the children among you reach puberty, they must ask permission, as those before them asked permission. Allah thus clarifies His revelations for you. Allah is Knowledgeable and Wise."
  * **Gloss:** Adulthood is precisely the age of knock-duty — "as those before them": the rule is one community across generations.
* **`[24:60]`** "Women past the age of childbearing, who have no desire for marriage, commit no wrong by taking off their outer clothing, provided they do not flaunt their finery. But to maintain modesty is better for them. Allah is Hearing and Knowing."
  * **Gloss:** The leniency-clause for elderly women: the outer garment ("outer clothing" — a garment-word distinct from 24:31's "coverings") may be set aside with no flaunting; the better-optional is restraint; the double name Hearing-Knowing polices speech and intent. [Correction logged: the task's "khimar/covering at 24:58" does not hold — 24:58 has no covering term.]
* **`[24:61]`** "There is no blame on the blind, nor any blame on the lame, nor any blame on the sick, nor on yourselves for eating at your homes, or your fathers’ homes, or your mothers’ homes, or your brothers’ homes, or your sisters’ homes, or the homes of your paternal uncles, or the homes of your paternal aunts, or the homes of your maternal uncles, or the homes of your maternal aunts, or those whose keys you own, or the homes of your friends. You commit no wrong by eating together or separately. But when you enter any home, greet one another with a greeting from Allah, blessed and good. Allah thus explains the revelations for you, so that you may understand."
  * **Gloss:** The open-tables charter: three medical exemptions and twelve categories of home — blood kin four ways, key-held houses, friends' houses; commensality needs no permission theater ("together or separately"); the only rite left standing is the greeting, described as "from Allah, blessed and good."

## 3. Key Terms & Roots
* **`occasions of privacy`** (thalathat ʿawrat): `[24:58]`.
  * **Rendering:** "three occasions of privacy" — the ʿawra-word's only time-schedule use; the related privacy noun does not recur in the corpus this way (rg-checked; cf. "screen" at 33:53 as the spatial kin).
  * **Elsewhere:** the permission verb-family returns at `[24:27–28]` ("asked permission"; "until you are given permission") and `[24:62]` — one root, three ruku of house-and-assembly law.
* **`outer clothing`** (an outer-garment noun; its precise Arabic identity is not decidable from this English corpus — *Source: classical Arabic — verify*): `[24:60]`.
  * **Rendering:** "taking off their outer clothing" — Itani's outer-garment noun, deliberately distinct from 24:31's "coverings" (khimar) over the bosom.
  * **Elsewhere:** `[33:59]` "to lengthen their garments" — the outer-garment dossier; whether 24:60's leniency touches the head-cover of 24:31 or only an overcloak is a classical fiqh dispute — *Source: classical fiqh — verify; the translation keeps the two garments as two words.*
* **`finery / beauty`** (zinah): `[24:60]` "flaunt their finery."
  * **Elsewhere:** `[24:31]` "not display their beauty except what is apparent" and `[33:33]` "do not display yourselves, as in the former days of ignorance" — the display-lexicon shared across the modesty verses.
* **`no blame`** (junah): `[24:60]`, `[24:61]` (and `[24:29]` first).
  * **Rendering:** the exemption-formula: "There is no blame on the blind, nor any blame on the lame, nor any blame on the sick."
  * **Elsewhere:** `[48:17]` "There is no blame on the blind, nor any blame on the lame, nor any blame on the sick" — word-identical triple, there for fighting, here for eating; `[9:91]` the cognate hardship exemption but a different triad ("the weak... the sick... those who have nothing to give") — the corpus's standing hardship-exemption family.
* **`maintain modesty`** (a self-restraint verb; classical Arabic *ista'faf* — not decidable from the English corpus — *Source: classical Arabic — verify*): `[24:60]`.
  * **Rendering:** "But to maintain modesty is better for them" — the "better/purer" comparison-formula (khayr/azka per classical Arabic — *Source: classical Arabic — verify*), cf. `[24:28]` "That is more proper for you" and `[24:30]` "That is purer for them."
* **`greeting from Allah`** (tahiyyat min ʿind Allah): `[24:61]`.
  * **Elsewhere:** `[24:27]` "greeted their occupants" — same ritual word; 24:61 upgrades it: the greeting's origin is divine, its quality "blessed and good."
