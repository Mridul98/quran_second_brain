# Two economies: infaq (circulating wealth) vs.riba (hoarding it)
**UID:** 202610132004
**Type:** Permanent / Domain Knowledge
**Source:** observable corpus facts in the cited `[s:a]` lines; the riba-destruction / charity-growth contrast is 2:276 verbatim; classical tafsir consensus treats 2:278–279 as the decisive prohibition (Ibn Kathir); the "two economies" synthesis is agent-structured — verify.

## Concept
The Quran reads money as theology made visible. Its counter-thesis to hoarding is a **divine business model**: wealth must flow toward God's named claimants or it dead-ends. The verbs are *infāq* ("spend in Allah's way," repeated as the community's signature act) and its metaphor engine: lending God a "good loan" that He multiplies "manyfold" (2:245, 57:18, 64:17). Against it stands *ribā* — guaranteed self-multiplication of capital — declared war against God and His Messenger (2:279), physically impossible ("will not rise except as one driven mad," 2:275), and economically inverted: "Allah condemns usury, and He blesses charities" (2:276), "usury... will not multiply with Allah" (30:39). Charity is audited by intent at the edges: 2:267 forbids giving "bad things you would not take yourselves," 3:92 makes spending the threshold of righteousness ("You will not attain virtuous conduct until you give of what you cherish"), and 63:10 makes it the last request of the dying (63:10 — "give from what We have provided you, before death approaches").

## Practical Reading
Any passage about wealth is a test between the two economies: **does this money move toward named others, or does it compound toward self?** Track the verb: infaq-verbs with God as counterparty appear most densely in 2:195–274, 3:76–92, 57:18–21, 64:16–17, 103; riba passages cluster at 2:275–279, 3:130 ("do not feed on usury, doubled and redoubled"), 4:161, and 30:39. The economics chapter of the book is one argument with two balance sheets.
